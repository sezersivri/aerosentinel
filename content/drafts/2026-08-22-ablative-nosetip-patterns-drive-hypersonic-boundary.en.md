**Type:** 🧪 Experimental | **Relevance:** 75/100

This paper experimentally investigates how the surface morphology resulting from ablation on a nose tip influences boundary-layer instability and transition in hypersonic flow. The authors designed and fabricated a conical nose tip with distributed roughness elements and random pores, mimicking the carbonized pattern of a pyrolytic composite used in thermal protection systems. Experiments were conducted in a Mach 6 hypersonic Ludwieg tube tunnel, employing high-frequency pressure sensors, focused laser differential interferometry, and infrared thermography to characterize the flow.

The key finding is that the ablative surface morphology significantly advances the onset position of second-mode instability waves. Infrared thermography further confirmed this transition-promoting effect. The authors attribute this influence to the nose-tip roughness elements, which advance the onset of instability waves, increase their linear growth rate, and promote an earlier transition to the nonlinear stage by modulating the receptivity process and distorting the mean flow.

Understanding the impact of realistic ablative surface patterns on boundary-layer transition is critical for accurate aerothermodynamic predictions and the design of thermal protection systems for high-speed missiles. This experimental work provides valuable insights into a complex flow phenomenon that directly affects aerodynamic heating loads.
