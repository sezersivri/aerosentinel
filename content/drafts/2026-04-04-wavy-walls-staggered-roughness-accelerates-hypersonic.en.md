This paper investigates the influence of three-dimensional wavy wall roughness topology on hypersonic boundary layer transition at Mach 5.92 using direct numerical simulations. The authors specifically compare two distribution types: staggered (type-S) and aligned (type-A) roughness, marking the first time these specific topologies have been directly contrasted for their transition-inducing effects.

The study found that type-S roughness significantly promotes earlier transition, occurring approximately 34.5% sooner than with type-A roughness under the conditions examined. Both roughness types generate counter-rotating pairs of streamwise vortices. However, a 'staggered-enhancing' mechanism was identified for type-S roughness, leading to substantially stronger vortices and subsequent stronger shear layers compared to type-A. Analysis using spectral proper orthogonal decomposition revealed that for type-S, downstream linear instability is dominated by a low-frequency shear layer instability near 50 kHz, which triggers nonlinear breakdown. For type-A, instability is co-dominated by a lower-frequency shear layer instability near 23 kHz and a high-frequency Mack's second mode above 100 kHz, both exhibiting lower growth rates than the dominant mode in the type-S case, thus delaying transition onset.

Understanding how surface roughness topology impacts boundary layer transition is critical for predicting aerodynamic heating on high-speed vehicles. The findings highlight that manufacturing tolerances or design choices leading to staggered roughness patterns could have a pronounced effect on the location and intensity of turbulent heating, which is a key consideration for thermal protection system design on hypersonic missiles.
