This study investigates supersonic combustion within a small-scale scramjet intake and combustor section, combining finite rate chemistry large-eddy simulations (LES) with experimental measurements. The research was conducted in the High Enthalpy Shock Tunnel Göttingen, simulating flight-relevant conditions including high-speed compressible flow, hydrogen injection, self-ignition, and turbulent combustion. The authors employed detailed models for hydrogen–air chemical kinetics and turbulence–chemistry interactions to accurately predict flame stabilization, heat release, and shock–turbulence interactions.

The simulations were validated against experimental data obtained from temperature-sensitive paint, pressure transducers, and frequency comb laser absorption spectroscopy. The results demonstrate good agreement between simulations and experiments for wall pressure, wall heat flux, and the emissions of carbon monoxide and water. The combined experimental and computational insights clarify flameholding mechanisms and highlight the role of shock waves in enhancing mixing and ignition within the combustor.

This work advances the fundamental understanding of supersonic combustion physics, providing a valuable framework for future scramjet engine studies relevant to realistic flight vehicles. While not directly focused on external missile aerothermodynamics, the detailed analysis of internal heat flux and high-enthalpy flow physics is relevant to the broader domain of high-speed vehicle thermal management.
