**Type:** 🧪 Experimental | **Relevance:** 75/100

This paper experimentally investigates how structural deformations, induced by aerothermal loads, influence surface heating on high-speed vehicles. The authors tested statically deflected panels, representing both simple single-mode and complex multi-mode deformations derived from prior fluid/thermal/structural interaction data, against a flat plate baseline. These experiments were conducted at Mach 6 in the U.S. Air Force Research Laboratory Ludwieg Tube, employing high-speed schlieren imaging for flow visualization and infrared thermography for heat transfer measurements.

The study found that while the deformations did not significantly alter boundary-layer instability modes as observed by schlieren, they had a substantial impact on heat transfer. Both deformed geometries delayed the onset of increased heating from boundary-layer transition, leading to a reduction in total integrated heat load at high Reynolds numbers compared to the flat plate. Conversely, at lower Reynolds numbers, localized flow compression caused by the panel curvature resulted in an increased heat load. The authors also developed two predictive models for estimating heat flux on arbitrarily deformed surfaces, which were then validated against their experimental data.

This work provides valuable experimental data on the complex interplay between structural deformation and aerothermal heating in hypersonic flow. Understanding these effects is critical for the accurate design and thermal management of hypersonic flight vehicles, particularly when considering the dynamic response of thermal protection systems to extreme environments.
