This paper investigates the influence of wall mass injection composition on the receptivity of a thermochemically nonequilibrium hypersonic blunt-cone boundary layer to free-stream slow acoustic waves. The authors perform direct numerical simulations (DNS) to analyze four distinct wall conditions: non-catalytic no injection, fully catalytic no injection, air injection, and pyrolysis-gas injection, aiming to understand how these conditions alter the boundary layer and its susceptibility to disturbances.

The simulations reveal that wall catalysis and air injection lead to a reduction in boundary layer thickness due to near-wall recombination, which modifies temperature and Mach number distributions. Conversely, pyrolysis-gas injection thickens the boundary layer, attributed to the high H2 content and subsequent increases in effective gas constant and thermal conductivity. These changes in the mean flow significantly alter the receptivity mechanism. For the non-catalytic no-injection case, the disturbance evolves from leading-edge modal synchronization to an unstable supersonic mode, culminating in downstream second-mode amplification. In contrast, the fully catalytic and air-injection cases are dominated by damped F2 modes. Pyrolysis-gas injection results in entropy/vorticity wave dominance in the intermediate region, followed by earlier second-mode excitation downstream. Frequency-dependent results, specifically between 700–1100 kHz, indicate that wall catalysis and air injection suppress unstable-mode growth, while high-frequency excitation is observed in the pyrolysis-gas case. The receptivity coefficient is highest for the non-catalytic no-injection scenario and lowest for the pyrolysis-gas-injection case among those exhibiting unstable-mode growth.

The findings demonstrate that both wall chemistry and the composition of the injected gas can regulate hypersonic boundary-layer receptivity by modifying the thermochemical mean flow and the pathways of dominant disturbance modes. This work provides insights into the fundamental physics of boundary layer transition under conditions relevant to ablative thermal protection systems.
