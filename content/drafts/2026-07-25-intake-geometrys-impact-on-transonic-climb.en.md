This paper presents an experimental investigation into how intake surface geometry affects performance during off-design climb conditions, specifically focusing on transonic flow over the lower lip of a civil aircraft intake. The study uses a quasi-two-dimensional experimental setup and examines 27 test cases within a design-of-experiments framework. Four independent parameters are varied: highlight curvature, surface-curvature distribution, diffuser shape, and mass flow rate. In each case, a transonic shock-wave–boundary-layer interaction forms on the intake front lip.

The key finding is that intake performance, quantified by incompressible boundary-layer displacement thickness ahead of the fan, is significantly degraded (by up to an order of magnitude) when this displacement thickness exceeds a certain threshold, indicating established separation. The leading-edge radius is identified as the most influential surface geometry parameter affecting shock strength and, consequently, intake performance during off-design climb. A smaller leading-edge radius increases initial subsonic turning, which reduces transonic region curvature and weakens the shock, leading to a more benign interaction with minimal impact on performance across the tested mass-flow range.

While this work provides valuable insights into transonic intake design for civil applications, its direct relevance to the prediction of aerodynamic heating on high-speed missiles using Gaussian process surrogate models is limited. The flow regime (transonic vs. hypersonic), physical phenomena of interest (intake performance vs. heating), and methodology (experimental vs. ML surrogates) are distinct from the core domain.
